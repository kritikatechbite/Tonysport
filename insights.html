Hockey

How the Power Play Works: A Fan's Guide

October 12, 2026 · Sample editorial

A clear walkthrough of penalties, man advantages and the formations teams use to take advantage of them.

Read More

When a player is penalized, their team plays shorthanded for two or five minutes, usually while the opposing team keeps a full complement. Most power plays use a five-player unit built around a shooter, a distributor and a net-front screen. Watch for how quickly the puck moves between the points and the half-wall: the goal is to pull defenders out of position and open a shooting lane. A minor penalty ends when a power-play goal is scored, while a major penalty runs its full length. Understanding that one rule explains many of the changes in momentum you will see in a close game.

Hockey

Icing and Offside, Explained Without the Jargon

October 10, 2026 · Sample editorial

Two rules that stop the play most often, and why they exist in the first place.

Read More

Offside prevents attacking players from waiting near the opposing net: the puck must enter the attacking zone before the players do. Icing stops a team from relieving pressure by simply shooting the puck the length of the rink. When icing is called, play returns to a face-off in the offending team's end, and the team cannot change lines. Leagues apply the details differently between hybrid and no-touch versions, so check the rulebook of the league you follow. Learning these two stoppages makes broadcasts far easier to follow.

Basketball

Reading the Pick and Roll

October 8, 2026 · Sample editorial

The most common action in modern basketball, broken into the choices each player makes.

Read More

In a pick and roll, an offensive player sets a screen for the ball handler and then moves toward the basket. The defence has several answers: switching, going under, going over, or dropping the big player back. Each choice trades a different risk, which is why watching the screener's defender tells you most of what is about to happen. Teams build entire offences around this simple action because it forces the defence to communicate under pressure.

Basketball

The Canadian Roots of Basketball

October 6, 2026 · Sample editorial

How a Canadian-born educator created a game that now has professional leagues around the world.

Read More

Basketball was created in 1891 by James Naismith, who was born in Ontario and was working as a physical education instructor in Massachusetts at the time. His original game used a soccer ball and two peach baskets, along with thirteen written rules. Today the sport is played in school gyms and community courts from coast to coast, and Canadian players compete at every level of the international game. Looking back at those origins gives Canadian fans a useful lens on how far the game has travelled.

Canadian Football

Three Downs: What Makes the Canadian Game Different

October 4, 2026 · Sample editorial

A side-by-side look at the field, the downs and the rules that shape Canadian football.

Read More

Canadian football gives the offence three downs, rather than four, to gain ten yards. The field is longer and wider, the end zones are deeper, and twelve players take the field per side. Receivers may be in motion toward the line of scrimmage before the snap, which creates more movement before every play. The single point, known as a rouge, can be scored on certain kicks that are not returned from the end zone. With fewer downs, teams tend to pass earlier and more often, which gives the game its fast, open style.

Canadian Football

Following the Road to the Grey Cup

October 2, 2026 · Sample editorial

How the regular season, playoffs and championship fit together for fans following the league.

Read More

The Grey Cup is the championship trophy of the Canadian Football League. Teams are organized into two divisions, and the division winners meet in the championship game after playoff rounds. Following the sport across a season is easiest by learning the standings, the bye week rules and how home-field advantage is decided. The league's own website and its broadcast partners publish current schedules and results, which this site does not replace.

Soccer

Soccer Basics: Offside, Set Pieces and Stoppage Time

September 30, 2026 · Sample editorial

The three ideas new fans ask about most, explained in plain language.

Read More

A player is offside when they are closer to the opposition goal line than both the ball and the second-last defender at the moment the ball is played to them, with some exceptions. Set pieces such as corners, free kicks and throw-ins create many scoring chances, so teams rehearse them carefully. Stoppage time, sometimes called added time, makes up for delays during a half and is decided by the referee. These three ideas explain most of the questions people have when they start watching.

Soccer

A Guide to the Canadian Soccer Pyramid

September 28, 2026 · Sample editorial

Where professional, semi-professional and community soccer fit together in Canada.

Read More

Canadian soccer includes professional clubs in several leagues, a semi-professional and amateur tier, and thousands of community clubs organized through provincial associations. Canada Soccer is the national governing body. Fans can follow men's, women's and youth competitions, and many cities have a supporter culture that extends far beyond the top flight. Because league structures change over time, check the official league websites for current membership.

A Plain-Language Glossary of Sports Terms

September 26, 2026 · Sample editorial

Fifteen common terms from several sports, defined in one paragraph each.

Read More

A hat trick means three goals by one player in a game. A clean sheet means a team did not concede any goals. A break point in tennis is a chance for the returner to win the server's game. The bullpen in baseball is where relief pitchers warm up. A power play is a man advantage following a penalty. A rouge is a one-point score in Canadian football. Knowing these terms makes it easier to follow commentary, news and our own guides on this site.

Explainers

How to Read a Sports Schedule Across Time Zones

September 24, 2026 · Sample editorial

Canada spans six time zones. Here is how to avoid missing a start time.

Read More

Canada has six time zones, from Pacific to Newfoundland. A listed start time is only useful if you know which zone it uses, so always check the label. Broadcast times and start times can differ, since pre-game coverage may begin earlier. Schedules can also change because of weather, venue availability or league decisions, so confirm details with the organizer before making plans.
